HS Codes Explained: How Tariff Classification Works (and How to Get It Right)

By Smart.Supply EditorialUpdated 2026-11-16⏱ 3 min read

What HS codes are, how the 6-digit international system extends to 8 and 10-digit national codes, the General Rules of Interpretation, binding rulings and common classification mistakes.

Every product that crosses a border is classified with a Harmonized System (HS) code. That code decides the duty rate you pay, whether licences or special rules apply, which trade statistics your shipment lands in and whether a free-trade agreement can cut your duty. Getting it wrong is one of the most common — and most expensive — import mistakes.

What the Harmonized System is

The HS is maintained by the World Customs Organization and used by more than 200 countries and economies. It organises goods into 21 sections, 96 chapters, about 1,200 headings and over 5,000 six-digit subheadings. The first six digits are the same everywhere. The current edition is HS 2022; the WCO revises the nomenclature roughly every five years, with the next edition planned for 2028.

How a code is built

DigitsLevelExample (cotton T-shirt)
2Chapter61 — knitted or crocheted apparel
4Heading6109 — T-shirts, singlets and vests, knitted
6Subheading (international)6109.10 — of cotton
8–10National tariff linesSet by each country

Countries add their own digits: the US Harmonized Tariff Schedule uses 10 digits, the EU Combined Nomenclature uses 8 (10 with TARIC measures), the UK and Canada use 10, and India's ITC-HS uses 8. The same product can therefore have the same first six digits but different full codes — and different duty rates — in each country.

The General Rules of Interpretation

Classification follows six General Rules of Interpretation (GRIs), applied in order:

  1. GRI 1: classify by the wording of the headings and the section and chapter notes. Most products are settled here.
  2. GRI 2: incomplete or unassembled goods are classified as the finished article if they have its essential character; mixtures are covered too.
  3. GRI 3: when goods could fall under two headings, use the most specific description, then essential character, then the heading that comes last numerically.
  4. GRI 4: goods that fit no heading go under the most similar goods.
  5. GRI 5: rules for cases, containers and packing materials.
  6. GRI 6: the same logic applies at subheading level.

How to find the right code

  1. Describe the product precisely: what it is, what it's made of, how it works and what it's used for.
  2. Search your destination country's tariff schedule and read the chapter notes, not just the headings.
  3. Check how customs has classified similar products — the US CROSS rulings database and the EU's EBTI database are public.
  4. For high-volume or ambiguous products, request a binding ruling (US) or Binding Tariff Information (EU). It gives legal certainty for years.
  5. Ask your customs broker to confirm before the first shipment, and keep your reasoning on file.

Common classification mistakes

  • Using the supplier's export code unchecked. The exporter's code is often wrong or only matches at six digits.
  • Classifying by use instead of by material where the chapter notes say otherwise.
  • Ignoring sets and kits. Retail sets follow GRI 3(b) essential-character rules.
  • Choosing a lower-duty code to save money. Customs can audit years back and add penalties.
  • Forgetting extra duties — anti-dumping, countervailing or special tariffs often apply to specific codes and origins.

Why it matters for your landed cost

A single digit can move duty from 0% to double digits. Once you have the code and rate, plug them into the landed cost calculator and compare sourcing countries — free-trade agreements and origin rules can change the answer. For the bigger picture, read Landed cost explained.

Classification is a legal determination. This guide is general information — confirm codes with a licensed customs broker or your customs authority.
