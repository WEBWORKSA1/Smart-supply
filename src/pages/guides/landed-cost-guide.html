Landed cost is the total cost of getting a product to your warehouse door: the purchase price plus every transport, insurance, customs, tax and handling cost. It is the number that should drive supplier selection and retail pricing — not the factory unit price.

The landed cost formula

Landed cost = Product cost + Freight + Insurance + Customs duty + Import taxes (non-recoverable) + Fees + Inland delivery

Divide by units to get landed cost per unit.

Every component explained

ComponentWhat it includes
Product costUnit price × quantity, plus tooling, samples, packaging upgrades
FreightOrigin charges, main carriage (ocean/air/road), destination terminal charges
InsuranceCargo insurance, often ~0.3–0.5% of cargo value depending on goods and route
Customs dutyRate by HS code × customs value (CIF basis in the EU/UK/India; FOB-like basis in the US/Canada)
Import taxesVAT/GST — often recoverable for registered businesses, so exclude if you reclaim it
FeesCustoms broker, port/terminal, documentation, bank/FX charges, inspections
Inland deliveryDrayage or trucking from port/airport to your warehouse

Worked example

You import 5,000 units at $2.40 each (goods value $12,000). Freight $1,800, insurance $60, duty 6.5% on CIF, broker and port fees $450, inland delivery $350.

  • CIF value = 12,000 + 1,800 + 60 = $13,860
  • Duty = 13,860 × 6.5% = $900.90
  • Total landed = 13,860 + 900.90 + 450 + 350 = $15,560.90
  • Landed cost per unit = $3.11 — about 30% above the factory price.

Why landed cost changes decisions

  • Supplier choice: a supplier 8% cheaper overseas can be more expensive landed than a nearshore supplier with lower freight and duty.
  • Pricing: margin calculated on factory price overstates profit; retail prices should be set from landed cost.
  • Shipping mode: moving from air to ocean can cut landed cost dramatically but adds weeks of inventory in transit.
  • Order size: larger orders spread fixed fees but raise holding costs — balance with EOQ.

Five ways to lower landed cost

  1. Correct HS classification — and check free-trade agreement eligibility (certificate of origin).
  2. Optimize carton sizes to improve container utilization (CBM calculator).
  3. Consolidate LCL shipments into FCL when volume passes roughly 13–15 CBM.
  4. Negotiate Incoterms — FCA with your own forwarder often beats supplier-arranged freight.
  5. Explore duty drawback and bonded warehousing for re-exported goods.
Duty rates and customs valuation rules vary by country and change frequently. Confirm with a licensed customs broker before relying on estimates.
