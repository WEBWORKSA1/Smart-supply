Tariff changes, pandemic-era disruptions and shipping shocks pushed "single-country sourcing" to the top of every risk register. China+1 keeps existing Chinese suppliers while adding at least one alternative country; nearshoring moves production closer to the end market. Both trade some unit-cost advantage for resilience and speed.

Why companies diversify

  • Tariff and trade-policy exposure concentrated on one origin.
  • Lead time: trans-Pacific ocean transit plus port dwell can mean 5–8 weeks door to door; trucking from Mexico to the US can be days.
  • Working capital: shorter lead times reduce inventory in transit and safety stock.
  • Customer and compliance requirements such as forced-labor rules and supplier traceability.

A 6-step transition plan

  1. Map spend and risk: rank SKUs by spend, margin and single-source exposure.
  2. Pick pilot SKUs: start with simple, high-volume parts with stable specs.
  3. Model total landed cost for each origin — freight, duty, lead time, inventory carrying cost (calculator).
  4. Qualify suppliers: audits, samples, first-article inspection (vetting checklist).
  5. Run dual sourcing for 2–3 cycles before shifting volume.
  6. Scale and renegotiate with incumbents using real competing quotes.

Hidden costs to budget

  • New tooling and molds (often $5k–$50k+ per part family).
  • Qualification travel, audits and third-party inspections.
  • Learning-curve yield losses in the first production runs.
  • Sub-component supply that may still originate in China.
