Importing directly from a manufacturer can cut product cost by 20–60% compared with buying from a domestic wholesaler — but only if you control quality, freight and customs. This playbook walks through the ten steps experienced importers follow, whether the factory is in China, India, Vietnam, Mexico or Türkiye.

Step 1: Write a specification before you contact anyone

A one-page spec sheet prevents most disputes. Include materials, dimensions and tolerances, colours (Pantone references), finishes, packaging and labelling, required certifications (CE, UL, FCC, FDA, CPSIA, REACH, depending on your market), target quantity and target price. Attach drawings or a reference sample photo. Suppliers quote more accurately — and more cheaply — when they don't have to guess.

Step 2: Build a long list of suppliers

Use B2B marketplaces (Alibaba, Global Sources, Made-in-China, IndiaMART), trade shows such as the Canton Fair, industry directories, sourcing agents, and referrals. Aim for 8–12 candidates, then cut to 3–6 after checking business licences, export history and whether each one is a factory or a trading company. Step 3: Send a structured RFQ

Ask every shortlisted supplier for the same things: unit price at three quantity tiers, MOQ, tooling or mould costs, sample cost, production lead time, packaging details, Incoterm and quote validity. Step 4: Order and approve samples

Pay for samples from your two or three best quotes. Test them against the spec, have certified labs test anything safety-related, and sign and photograph the approved "golden sample". Keep one copy yourself and leave one with the factory — it becomes the quality reference for production.

Step 5: Negotiate and sign a purchase contract

Negotiate the whole package, not just price: payment terms, lead time, defect allowance, packaging, penalties for late delivery, and who owns the moulds. Put everything in a written purchase order or manufacturing agreement, including the Incoterm with a named place (for example "FCA Shenzhen, Incoterms® 2020") and your right to inspect. Protect your designs with a non-disclosure, non-use agreement before sharing sensitive drawings.

Step 6: Pay safely

The industry norm for new relationships is a 30% deposit and 70% balance — ideally released against a copy of the bill of lading, after inspection. Use letters of credit or marketplace escrow for larger first orders. Always confirm bank details by phone; changed-bank-details emails are the most common fraud in importing. Step 7: Inspect before you pay the balance

Book a third-party pre-shipment inspection when goods are 80–100% packed. Inspectors draw a statistical sample (AQL) and check it against your spec and golden sample. For complex products, add a during-production inspection at 20–50% completion so defects are caught while they can still be fixed.

Step 8: Book freight and choose the mode

Under your Incoterm, either the supplier or your freight forwarder books transport. Small, urgent or high-value goods suit air or express; most volume moves by sea — LCL for small loads, FCL once you fill roughly half a container. Compare options in Freight modes compared and size your shipment with the CBM calculator.

Step 9: Clear customs

You (or your customs broker) declare the goods, classify them under the correct tariff code and pay duties and import taxes. Requirements differ by country. For example, US importers of record usually need a customs bond for formal entries (generally shipments valued over $2,500) and must file an Importer Security Filing ("10+2") at least 24 hours before ocean cargo is loaded abroad. The US also ended duty-free de minimis treatment for most low-value shipments in 2025, so check current rules before shipping samples or small orders. Classification drives your duty rate — read HS codes explained.

Step 10: Calculate true landed cost — and plan around holidays

Add freight, insurance, duty, import taxes, broker and port fees and inland delivery to the factory price. The difference is often 20–40%. Use the landed cost calculator before you set retail prices. Finally, plan around Lunar New Year: many Chinese and Vietnamese factories close for two to four weeks around it (6 February in 2027), so orders for spring stock should be confirmed by early December.
